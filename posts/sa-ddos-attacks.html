South Africa Under Siege: Breaking Down the Massive DDoS Campaign

19 May 2026 8 min read Eugene Myburgh

So this literally happened today. Multiple South African internet companies got hit by a coordinated DDoS attack campaign — and not small ones either. We're talking attacks peaking at 1 Tbps. I wanted to break down what happened, what techniques the attackers used, and what this means for anyone trying to understand how DDoS attacks actually work.

What Happened?

Starting on Monday 19 May, a wave of massive DDoS attacks started slamming into South African internet infrastructure. This wasn't just one company getting hit — it was a coordinated campaign targeting multiple providers at the same time:

  • Seacom — undersea cable operator, hit on Tuesday morning causing widespread connectivity issues
  • Host Africa — attack peaked at a staggering 1 Tbps
  • Domains.co.za — hit with attacks reaching 100 Gbps
  • 1-Grid — reported attacks exceeding 100 Gbps across their IP ranges
  • Liquid Intelligent Technologies — had to shut down UDP traffic as attacks hit 90 Gbps
  • Xneelo — reported network degradation on Tuesday morning, later confirmed DDoS
  • Network Platforms — sustained attack traffic averaging 300 Gbps, peaking at 675 Gbps

The collateral damage was massive. News sites like BusinessTech and News24 experienced outages, government services had connectivity issues, and Downdetector lit up like a Christmas tree across platforms.

Scale check: To put 1 Tbps in perspective — that's 1,000 Gbps. The average South African home connection is around 50-100 Mbps. This attack was the equivalent of roughly 10,000 home connections worth of traffic being blasted at a single target simultaneously.

Wait, What Is a DDoS Attack?

If you're new to this like me, let me break it down. DDoS stands for Distributed Denial of Service. The idea is simple:

  • Imagine a shop with one door that can handle 10 customers at a time
  • Now imagine 10,000 people all trying to shove through that door at once
  • The real customers can't get in — the shop is effectively shut down
  • That's a DDoS attack, but instead of people, it's network traffic flooding a server

The "Distributed" part means the attack traffic comes from thousands of sources (usually compromised devices called a botnet), making it way harder to block than if it came from a single IP address.

How Did They Do It?

This is the interesting part. The attackers didn't just use one method — they combined multiple techniques to make the attacks harder to defend against. Here's what was reported:

IP Fragmentation

When data travels across the internet, large packets get split into smaller fragments and reassembled at the destination. Attackers exploit this by sending tons of broken or overlapping fragments. The target's server wastes resources trying to reassemble garbage data, or the fragments are crafted to bypass firewall rules that only inspect the first fragment.

Carpet Bombing

Instead of hammering one IP address (which is easier to detect and block), carpet bombing spreads the attack traffic across an entire IP range. Each individual IP gets a relatively low amount of traffic — often below detection thresholds — but the total volume across all IPs is massive. Traditional DDoS defences that look for spikes on single IPs often miss this completely.

Why it's clever: Carpet bombing is like death by a thousand cuts. No single target looks like it's under attack, but the upstream links and shared infrastructure get overwhelmed. That's why so many different companies and services went down — the shared pipes were saturated.

DNS Application Attacks & DNS Water Torture

This one is nasty. Instead of just flooding bandwidth, DNS Water Torture targets the DNS infrastructure itself — the system that translates domain names (like google.com) to IP addresses.

The attacker sends thousands of DNS queries for random subdomains that don't exist (like asd8f7asdf.target.com). The DNS server can't find them in its cache, so it has to do a full lookup for every single one. Multiply that by millions of requests and the DNS server drowns — taking down every website and service that depends on it.

Example DNS Water Torture queries 
# What DNS Water Torture queries look like:
# Each one is a random subdomain that forces a full lookup

dig aJ7kx9mQ.target.co.za
dig bR3nL8pW.target.co.za
dig xK9vM2cD.target.co.za
dig qW5tY7hN.target.co.za
dig fL1sP4jB.target.co.za

# Multiply this by millions per second...
# The DNS server can't keep up.

The Smokescreen Theory

Here's something that made me think. Security experts pointed out that DDoS attacks are sometimes used as a smokescreen — a distraction while attackers carry out something else in the background, like a data breach or ransomware deployment.

Think about it: if your entire security team is scrambling to deal with a massive DDoS attack, who's watching the logs for a quiet lateral movement or data exfiltration happening at the same time?

Something to watch: It'll be worth keeping an eye on the news over the next few weeks. If any of these companies report data breaches or compromises, it could suggest the DDoS was just the opening act.

Who's Doing This and Why?

The honest answer? Nobody knows yet. Cybersecurity firm Netscout told MyBroadband that the scale and coordination of the attacks point to a targeted campaign with a clear objective, but identifying the attackers is difficult unless they directly communicate with the target.

Possible motivations include:

  • Ideological / Hacktivism — political or social motivations targeting SA infrastructure
  • Extortion — "pay us or the attacks continue" (known as ransom DDoS / RDDoS)
  • Criminal distraction — the smokescreen theory mentioned above
  • Competitive sabotage — less likely at this scale, but not impossible

Netscout also warned that companies should prepare for subsequent waves or changes in attack technique. Attackers often adapt when defenders put up mitigations, switching from volumetric floods to application-layer attacks or vice versa.

What I Learned From This

  • DDoS isn't just "lots of traffic" — modern attacks combine multiple techniques (volumetric, fragmentation, application-layer) to overwhelm different parts of the defence stack
  • Carpet bombing is sneaky — spreading attack traffic below per-IP thresholds is a clever way to bypass traditional detection
  • DNS is a single point of failure — take out DNS and everything goes down, even if the actual servers are fine
  • Collateral damage is real — when shared infrastructure gets hit, everyone connected to it suffers, not just the target
  • Always ask "what else?" — a DDoS attack might not be the real attack
  • Dedicated DDoS protection matters — Seacom noted that customers using their DDoS protection services were unaffected
Bottom line: This was a serious, coordinated campaign against South African internet infrastructure. Whether it's hacktivism, extortion, or something else — it's a reminder that SA is very much a target, and the attacks are getting bigger and more sophisticated. If you're in the industry, now's the time to review your DDoS mitigation strategy.
